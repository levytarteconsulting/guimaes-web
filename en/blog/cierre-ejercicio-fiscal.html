2026 year-end close: what to decide before December 31

GGUIMAES · November 18, 2026 · 5 min read

In short

  • In 2027 Corporate Income Tax rates fall for micro-companies and small companies.
  • That is why this year bringing expenses forward and deferring income is worth more than usual, always within the timing rules.
  • The capitalisation and levelling reserves reduce the taxable base and must be decided before year-end.
  • Size-based reduced rates do not apply to asset-holding companies.
  • The first step is to estimate the year's result: without that figure nothing can be decided.

This year's close has a twist that last year's did not: in 2027 Corporate Income Tax rates fall. That changes the arithmetic of what is worth doing in December.

On December 31 your result is locked in. From January 1 you can file, but you can no longer decide. And the money between one and the other is usually far more than people imagine.

The usual still applies: review depreciation, deductions, balances, inventory. But this year there is an extra layer.

Why this year-end close is different

Corporate Income Tax rates are in the middle of a gradual reduction. These are the 2026 rates and where they are heading:

Type of entity20262027
General25%25%
Micro-companies (turnover under €1M)19% on the first €50,000 of taxable base, 21% on the rest17% and 20%
Small companies (€1M to €10M)23%22%, falling one point a year to 20% in 2029
Newly created entities15% in the first year with profits and the next one15%

If your company is a micro-company or a small company, the same euro of profit pays less tax in 2027 than in 2026. That has two practical consequences for this December:

  • An expense deducted in 2026 saves you more than the same expense deducted in 2027, because it comes off a base taxed at a higher rate.
  • Income that accrues in 2027 pays less than if it accrues in 2026.

Both point the same way: this year, bringing expenses forward and deferring income is worth more than usual. Always within the timing rules, of course: this is not about placing an invoice wherever it suits you, but about deciding when you do something you were going to do anyway.

An important caveat: size-based reduced rates do not apply to asset-holding companies. If your company does not carry on a business activity, this part does not apply to you.

The two tools almost nobody uses

Beyond bringing things forward or pushing them back, there are two tools designed precisely for this. They are legal, they have been in the law for years and they are hugely underused.

Capitalisation reserve

If you increase your equity and maintain that increase, you can deduct 20% of the increase from your taxable base.

  • The increase must be maintained for three years and a restricted reserve set aside for the same amount.
  • The general limit is 20% of the taxable base, rising to 25% for micro-companies.
  • And if you also increase your average headcount and keep it for three years, the percentage rises to 23%, 26.5% or 30% depending on the increase.

In practice: if you have made a profit this year and are not going to distribute all of it, leaving it in the company can pay off twice over.

Levelling reserve

Small companies and micro-companies can deduct up to 10% of their positive taxable base, up to a maximum of €1M a year.

That amount is offset against losses in the following five years. If there are none, it is added back to the base when that period ends. In other words, you bring forward the offset of future losses.

Put plainly: it is a deferral, not a gift. But if you expect a weak year soon, or simply want to improve this year's cash position, it is a very useful tool.

Of all of them, the first two give the most room to manoeuvre, and they are the two that have to be done now, not in January.

The most common mistake

It is not getting a box wrong. It is reaching January without having looked at anything.

What happens then is always the same: deductions lost because something had to be done before the 31st, real expenses that cannot be supported because nobody asked for the invoice in time, and mismatches that turn the July return into a far more expensive job than it needed to be.

An orderly close is not just about paying less. It also means a much calmer first quarter.

Frequently asked questions

When should I start preparing the close?+

Ideally in November, with an estimate of the year's result. There is still room in December, but it narrows: many decisions need time to carry out, not just to make.

Does bringing expenses forward always reduce tax?+

No. It only makes sense if it is an expense you were going to need anyway. Spending €10,000 to save €2,100 in tax means losing €7,900. This year, it is also worth comparing the 2026 rate with the 2027 rate before deciding.

Does this apply to sole traders too?+

Yes. Sole traders also lock in their result on December 31, although the tools are different: there is no capitalisation or levelling reserve, but there are decisions on expenses, investments and pension plan contributions.

What if my company is an asset-holding company?+

Size-based reduced rates do not apply to you: you pay tax at 25%. The part of this article on the rate cuts does not apply, but the rest of the checks do.

What happens if I review nothing before closing?+

You will probably pay more than necessary. And fixing it afterwards, where that is possible, costs more than planning it would have.
