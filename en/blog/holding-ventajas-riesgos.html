Holding company: pros, risks and when it pays

GGUIMAES · October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • A holding company is a company whose main purpose is to own shares in other companies.
  • Its great advantage is being able to reinvest subsidiaries' profits without first passing through the shareholders' personal income tax.
  • It also organises the group, ring-fences risks and makes it easier to bring in partners or plan succession.
  • It has running costs and must respond to a genuine business reason, not just a tax one.
  • It pays off when there are profits to reinvest, several activities or assets to protect.

Setting up a holding company is one of the most common structural decisions among business owners who already have a successful company. It is also one of the most often considered for the wrong reasons. Well designed, it organises the group, protects assets and allows more efficient reinvestment. Poorly planned, it adds costs and risks without contributing anything. In this article we explain what it is, its benefits, the risks worth knowing and when it is worth it.

What a holding company is

A holding company is a company, usually a limited company, whose main activity is owning shares in other companies, usually called subsidiaries. Instead of the business owner being a direct shareholder in each of their companies, they own the holding, and the holding owns the rest.

A distinction is usually drawn between a pure holding, which simply owns and manages shareholdings, and a mixed holding, which also provides services to the subsidiaries —management, administration, financing— or carries on its own business. The difference is not just theoretical: it affects how the holding is treated for tax purposes and which advantages it can use.

Benefits of setting up a holding company

Reinvesting without going through personal income tax

This is the best-known advantage. When a business owner receives dividends from their company as an individual, they are taxed in their personal income tax. If a holding company receives those dividends, and the shareholding and holding-period requirements set by law are met, they can be largely exempt from Corporate Tax. The result is that the money reaches the holding almost intact and can be reinvested in another business, in property or in financial investments without first being taxed as the shareholder's income.

The same applies, with some nuances, to capital gains the holding makes when selling shares in a subsidiary. It is important to understand that this is not a permanent saving but a deferral: when money leaves the holding for the shareholders, it will be taxed in their personal income tax.

Ring-fencing risks

If you have several activities, keeping them in separate companies under a holding prevents a problem in one from dragging down the others. The same goes for assets: property or accumulated cash can sit in a company separate from the one that bears the business risk.

Organising the group, new partners and succession

A holding makes it easier to bring a partner or investor into just one of the activities without touching the rest, centralises decision-making and simplifies succession planning in a family business, because what is passed on are shares in a single company. If there are several partners, it is the moment to set out their relationship properly in a shareholder agreement.

Managing the group as a whole

When the holding owns a very high stake in its subsidiaries, the group may be taxed under the tax consolidation regime, offsetting the profits of some companies against the losses of others. The holding can also centralise shared services and group financing, provided transactions between companies are valued at market price.

Risks and costs worth knowing

  • Running costs. One more company means more bookkeeping, more annual accounts, more tax returns and more administration. If the expected saving is small, the costs can wipe it out.
  • Valid business reason. The transactions used to create a holding, such as contributing your companies' shares to it, can be tax-neutral, but only if they respond to a genuine business reason. If the Tax Agency concludes the only aim was to save tax, it can deny that neutrality.
  • Holding with no activity. A holding that simply accumulates assets without genuinely managing its shareholdings or carrying on any business can lose important advantages, for example in wealth and inheritance taxation.
  • Related-party transactions. Loans, services or rentals between the holding, its subsidiaries and the shareholders must be documented and valued at market price. It is one of the areas the Tax Agency reviews most.
  • Rigidity. Undoing a badly designed structure costs more than thinking it through properly from the start.

When it pays off and when it doesn't

A holding company usually makes sense when one or more of these situations apply:

  • Your company generates profits you don't need to live on and you want to reinvest them in other projects.
  • You have, or will have, several activities or businesses with different risks.
  • You want to separate accumulated assets from the risk of the business.
  • You are going to bring partners or investors into part of the business.
  • You are planning the succession of a family business.

On the other hand, if your company distributes everything it earns because you need it to live, if you don't plan to reinvest or diversify, or if profits are small, a holding will probably bring you more work than benefits. And if you are considering it as part of a wider reorganisation, you may want to read when corporate restructuring makes sense.

How it is set up, step by step

  • Preliminary analysis. Current situation, goals, expected profits and a simulation of the tax burden with and without a holding.
  • Structure design. Which companies sit under the holding, who owns what and how functions are divided.
  • Incorporation and contribution. The holding is normally set up by contributing the shares of the existing companies, documenting the business reason for the transaction.
  • Getting it running. Accounting, tax obligations, agreements between companies and, where relevant, intra-group service contracts.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to maintain a holding company?+

It depends on its activity and the number of companies, but it always involves its own bookkeeping, annual accounts and tax returns. That is why it is worth comparing that cost with the expected saving before setting it up.

Can I set up a holding if I only have one company?+

Yes. In fact it is common: the holding becomes the owner of your current company and receives its profits to reinvest them. What matters is that there is a business reason, such as reinvesting or separating assets.

Does a holding eliminate tax on dividends?+

It doesn't eliminate it, it defers it. Dividends can reach the holding with almost no tax if the requirements are met, but when money passes from the holding to the shareholders it will be taxed in their personal income tax.

What happens if the Tax Agency considers the holding only aims to save tax?+

It can deny the tax neutrality of the transactions used to create it. That is why it is important to document the business reason for the structure from the start.

Does a holding protect my personal assets?+

It helps separate business assets from the risk of each activity, but it doesn't replace other protection measures. We cover this in detail in our article on separating personal and business finances.
