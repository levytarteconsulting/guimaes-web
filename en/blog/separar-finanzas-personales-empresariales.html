Separating personal and business finances

GGUIMAES · October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Mixing personal and business money complicates the accounts and exposes you to the Tax Agency.
  • Separate accounts and cards are the first step, not the only one.
  • Set yourself a regular income and document any movement between you and your company.
  • Asset protection depends on the legal form, the guarantees you sign and how you act.
  • Good separation also lets you know whether your business really makes money.

In many small businesses, the owner's money and the company's money end up in the same pocket. The shopping is paid with the company card, a cash gap is covered from the personal account, money is withdrawn when needed. It works until it doesn't: when the Tax Agency asks questions, when you need financing, when a partner joins or when the business goes through a rough patch. Properly separating the two is one of the simplest and most profitable measures you can take.

Why separating them matters

  • To know whether your business makes money. If personal expenses are mixed with business ones, neither the profit and loss account nor the cash position tells you the truth.
  • To avoid problems with the Tax Agency. A personal expense deducted as a business one can be adjusted with surcharges and interest, and in a company it may also be treated as pay to the partner.
  • To protect your assets. If you set up a company to limit your liability, mixing assets weakens exactly that separation.
  • To grow. Banks, investors and future partners look for clean accounts. Mixed accounts reduce credibility and value.

If you are a sole trader

As a sole trader, the law makes no distinction between your assets and your business's: you are liable for business debts with all your property. That makes order even more important. Even if you aren't required to have a separate bank account, having one makes bookkeeping, quarterly returns and defending your expenses in a review much easier.

If your activity grows or takes on significant risks, it is worth asking whether it still makes sense to operate as an individual. We help you decide in sole trader or limited company?.

If you have a company

A company is a separate person from you, with its own assets. Its money isn't yours even if you are the sole shareholder, and you can only take it out with a clear basis: salary, dividend, repayment of a documented loan or payment for a service at market price. Any other movement creates a balance between you and the company that has to be explained and settled.

How you should draw income from your company has a direct impact on your personal tax; we explain it in company structure and your income tax return.

Signs your finances are mixed up

If you recognise yourself in two or more of these situations, it is worth putting things in order as soon as possible:

  • You don't know how much you have drawn from your business this year.
  • Every quarter your adviser asks about payments and you don't know whether they were personal or business.
  • You cover the company's cash gaps from your personal account, or vice versa, without keeping a record.
  • Your company's balance sheet shows a balance with the partner that nobody can explain.
  • You couldn't say whether the business, on its own, is profitable.

Seven practical measures

  • Separate accounts and cards. One account for the business and another for you, with different cards. It is the basis for everything else.
  • A fixed income. Pay yourself a regular amount, as if you were an employee. It forces you to plan and avoids withdrawals on demand.
  • Nothing personal from the company. If you pay something personal from the business account by mistake, record it and pay it back, rather than leaving it as an expense.
  • Loans in writing. If you lend money to your company, or the other way round, put it in a contract with the amount, term and a market interest rate.
  • Mixed expenses with a clear rule. Vehicle, phone or utilities you use for both: define which share belongs to the business and document it.
  • A cushion on each side. The company needs its own contingency fund, and so do you. If one always depends on the other, the separation isn't real. We cover this in cash flow and liquidity.
  • Regular review. Once a quarter, check with your adviser that there are no outstanding balances between you and the company.

Asset protection: beyond the accounts

Having a company generally limits your liability for business debts. But that protection has limits worth knowing:

  • Personal guarantees. If you personally guarantee a company loan or lease, you are personally liable for that debt.
  • Director liability. Failing to meet certain legal duties, for example in the face of serious losses, can make you personally liable.
  • Commingled assets. If in practice there is no difference between your money and the company's, the legal separation loses strength.
  • Matrimonial property regime. It affects which assets may be exposed to business debts. It is worth reviewing with a professional.

Beyond a certain level of assets or activity, structures such as a holding company or suitable liability insurance complete the protection. We explain it in holding company: pros, risks and when it pays.

Frequently asked questions

Does a sole trader have to have a separate bank account?+

It isn't compulsory, but it is highly recommended: it simplifies bookkeeping and makes it easy to justify your income and expenses.

Can I take money out of my company whenever I need it?+

Only with a clear basis: salary, dividend, loan repayment or payment for a service. Unjustified withdrawals create balances that have to be settled.

What if I have paid personal expenses through the company?+

They should be recorded as a balance you owe and repaid, or treated as pay. Leaving them as business expenses may end in an adjustment.

Does a limited company always protect my personal assets?+

It generally limits your liability, but it doesn't cover personal guarantees you sign or certain breaches of your duties as a director.

Can I lend money to my own company?+

Yes, and it is common. Do it with a contract setting the amount, term and a market interest rate, and record it in the accounts.
