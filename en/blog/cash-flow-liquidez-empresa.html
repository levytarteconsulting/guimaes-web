Cash flow and liquidity: what every growing company must control

GGUIMAES · June 9, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Profit is not the same as cash: you can earn money and not have it available.
  • Growth consumes cash: more sales usually require more stock and more customer credit.
  • Collecting sooner and paying in an orderly way matters as much as selling more.
  • A 13-week cash forecast anticipates problems with room to react.
  • Cash control is the first job of a CFO, internal or outsourced.

Contents

  1. Profit vs cash: why they are not the same
  2. Why growth can drown you
  3. The levers to improve liquidity
  4. The 13-week cash forecast

A company can be profitable on paper and still run out of money to pay salaries. That paradox has a name: lack of liquidity. Profit is measured in the income statement, but what lets you operate is cash. In this article we explain the difference between profit and cash flow, why growing companies are especially vulnerable and how to control your treasury so you avoid nasty surprises.

Profit vs cash: why they are not the same

Your income statement records a sale when you issue the invoice, even if the client pays in 60 or 90 days. Meanwhile, you have already paid your suppliers, your team and the tax office. That gap between what you have earned and what you have in the bank is the root of almost every liquidity problem. Profit tells you whether the business works; cash tells you whether it survives this month.

Why growth can drown you

The more you grow, the more cash you need up front: you buy more raw materials, hold more stock, extend more credit to clients and hire before collecting. This is so-called 'profitable but unsustainable growth': the business is doing well, but cash does not keep up. That is why many companies hit a squeeze precisely when they are selling the most.

The levers to improve liquidity

  • Collect sooner: clear terms, prompt invoicing and follow-up on late payers.
  • Negotiate payment terms with suppliers without harming the relationship.
  • Match stock to real demand so you do not tie up cash.
  • Plan tax payments, which are predictable cash-outflow peaks.
  • Have financing lines arranged before you need them.

The 13-week cash forecast

The most useful tool for an SME is not a complex model but a 13-week cash forecast: what comes in and what goes out, week by week, over the next quarter. It warns you in advance of the week cash would turn negative, so you can act today —speed up a collection, delay a purchase, draw on a line— instead of discovering it the day you cannot pay.

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between cash flow and profit?+

Profit is the difference between income and expenses according to your accounts, even if you have not collected or paid. Cash flow is the real money coming into and out of the bank. You can have profit and, at the same time, a lack of cash.

How often should I review my treasury?+

At least weekly if you are growing or under strain. The 13-week forecast is updated weekly to reflect real collections and payments against what was planned.

Do I need a CFO to control cash?+

Not necessarily full-time. Many SMEs solve this with an outsourced CFO a few hours a month, who sets up the system, reviews it with you and warns you before there is a problem.

What do I do if I foresee a month without liquidity?+

The sooner you see it, the more options you have: speed up collections, renegotiate a payment, use a credit line or adjust a purchase. The mistake is discovering it on the day; the solution is foreseeing it weeks ahead.
