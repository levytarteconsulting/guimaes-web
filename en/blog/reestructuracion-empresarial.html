Corporate restructuring: when it makes sense and what steps it takes

GGUIMAES · June 17, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Restructuring can be offensive (grow, separate, bring in a partner) or defensive (rescue).
  • There is corporate, financial and operational restructuring; they often combine.
  • Well-designed operations may qualify for the tax neutrality regime.
  • It needs a valid economic reason; doing it only for tax reasons is risky.
  • Order matters: diagnosis, design, legal execution and follow-up.

Restructuring a company is not a synonym for crisis. Sometimes you reorganise to grow in an orderly way, to separate activities, to make it easier for a partner to come in or to optimise a group's taxation. Other times, yes, to save a strained business. In every case, a poorly planned restructuring destroys value; a well-planned one protects and multiplies it. Here we explain when it makes sense and what steps it requires.

When does restructuring make sense?

  • You want to separate a risky activity from the main assets.
  • You are bringing in a partner or investor and need a clear structure.
  • You have several disorganised companies that should be simplified or grouped.
  • You are preparing a sale or a generational handover.
  • The business has debt strain and needs to reorder its liabilities.

Types of restructuring

Corporate restructuring reorganises ownership and companies: mergers, spin-offs, contributions of business branches or the creation of a holding company. Financial restructuring reorders debt and capital structure: refinancing, capitalisations or new funds. Operational restructuring touches the business itself: processes, costs, activity lines and team. In practice, a project usually combines all three. On the financial side, having financial management in place —in-house or an outsourced CFO— helps prepare the figures that banks and investors will ask for.

The tax factor: neutrality and economic reason

Many restructuring operations can use a special tax neutrality regime that avoids taxation at the moment of reorganisation. But there is a key condition: there must be a valid economic reason beyond tax saving. Designing the operation only for tax reasons is the fast track to the tax office challenging it. That is why the legal and tax sides should be coordinated from the start.

The steps of an orderly restructuring

  1. Diagnosis: understand the why, the starting point and the goal.
  2. Design of the optimal structure, coordinating legal, tax and financial aspects.
  3. Legal execution: agreements, deeds, registrations and notifications.
  4. Integration and follow-up: making the new structure work day to day.

Frequently asked questions

Does restructuring mean the company is doing badly?+

Not necessarily. Many restructurings are offensive: done to grow in an orderly way, separate activities, bring in a partner or prepare a sale. Only some respond to difficult situations.

Can you restructure without paying tax?+

Many operations can use the tax neutrality regime, which defers taxation, provided there is a valid economic reason. It is neither 'free' nor automatic: it must be designed and justified properly.

How long does a restructuring take?+

It depends on complexity and the type of operation. A simple corporate reorganisation can be resolved in weeks; a project with several companies, debt and partners can take several months.

Do I need to coordinate a lawyer and a tax advisor?+

Yes, and it is where projects most often fail. When legal and tax work separately, inconsistencies and avoidable costs appear. Having it all in one team simplifies and protects the operation.
